Designing an SNCR retrofit for a biomass boiler

A four-person design study: can selective non-catalytic reduction bring a biomass boiler under its NOx limit, and at what cost?

At a glance

Problem
An upgraded biomass boiler must stay under the 300 mg/Nm³ NOx limit, and its modelled uncontrolled NOx sat around that limit. Could an SNCR retrofit give a safe margin, and at what cost?
My contribution
Carried out the literature survey, built the Python (Cantera) combustion and SNCR kinetic models, did the hand calculations for the design basis and contributed the Aspen HYSYS simulation. Presented the poster in Oslo.
Key result
With a conservative design reduction of about 60% (the ideal model gave above 90%), modelled emissions fall well under the limit. Capital and running costs were estimated: not profitable at current NOx tax, but justified as a compliance cost.
Limits
Idealised model (perfect mixing, fixed temperature profile), very limited plant data for comparison, and a design study rather than a plant trial. On-site measurement was recommended.

Problem and baseline

An existing biomass boiler at a Norwegian industrial site is to be upgraded to supply process steam to a planned pulp mill. Burning biomass produces NOx, and the plant has to stay under a limit of 300 mg/Nm³. The team asked whether a selective non-catalytic reduction (SNCR) retrofit, injecting ammonia into the hot flue gas, could keep it there with a safe margin.

Baseline. The combustion models put uncontrolled NOx around the limit for both fuel cases studied, which is the range where SNCR is known to work. Only one older plant measurement was available for comparison.

My contribution

I was one of four authors of the design report. My part:

  • Literature survey on SNCR chemistry, reagents and operating windows.
  • The Python (Cantera) models: equilibrium combustion and the one-dimensional plug-flow SNCR kinetics, mapping NO reduction and ammonia slip against injection temperature and ammonia dose.
  • Hand calculations for the design basis, and the Aspen HYSYS process simulation.
  • Presenting the work as a poster at Treforedlingsforum 2025 in Oslo, 18–19 November.

Reagent selection, equipment sizing, safety measures and the cost estimates were the team’s joint work.

Methods and assumptions

  1. Checked the starting point

    Spreadsheet calculations estimated the flame temperature and the time the flue gas spends in the post-combustion zone, to see whether the boiler suits SNCR.

  2. Modelled the combustion

    A Cantera equilibrium model with the GRI-Mech 3.0 mechanism, with the solid fuels represented by a gas-phase surrogate from their ultimate analysis, predicted flue-gas temperature and uncontrolled NO for two feed cases.

  3. Modelled the SNCR

    A one-dimensional plug-flow reactor in Python with Cantera and the San Diego Reduced-N mechanism tracked NO reduction and ammonia slip for different injection temperatures and ammonia-to-NOx ratios (NSR).

  4. Designed and costed the system

    Ammonia was chosen as the reagent for performance and cost. The team laid out storage, mixing and injection, sized the main equipment, set out safety measures and estimated capital and running costs.

The design cases are not an independent prediction: the report tuned the model to about 60% reduction, which it describes as consistent with industrial experience, by running it at an unrealistically high 1400 °C, where modelled ammonia slip is zero. The report calls this optimistic. Absolute emission levels, fuel flows and plant data are not shown here because they depend on the industry partners’ information.

  • Temperature matters most. Reduction is limited below about 850 °C, rises quickly between 900 and 1000 °C and falls at higher temperatures as ammonia oxidises. At the cooler end of the window modelled ammonia slip rises sharply, so the recommendation is to inject between 900 and 1100 °C, near the upper end.
  • Reagent and cost. Reagent use dominates the running cost. Capital and running costs were estimated with standard cost correlations and public prices. At current NOx tax rates the project is not profitable, but the report judges the investment justified as a compliance measure.
  • Safety was part of the design. Ammonia is hazardous, so the design included a moat around the tank, ammonia sensors with alarms that switch off the pumps, emergency showers and enclosed mixing and distribution modules.
  • Recommendations. Measure temperature and uncontrolled NOx on site before tuning, and design for variations in load and fuel.

Limitations

  • Idealised models. Perfect mixing, a fixed temperature profile and steady state make the SNCR results an upper bound; real furnaces have imperfect mixing and temperature variation, and real ammonia slip is likely to be higher.
  • The 60% is an assumption. It is a conservative design value chosen from industrial experience, not a result the model produced.
  • Thin plant data. The baseline came from models, with only one older plant measurement for comparison.
  • High-level costs. The cost estimates are early-stage, and the report notes that some equipment costs are less accurate than the storage tank estimate.
  • A design study. Nothing here was tested on the boiler.

What I took from it. This was my first design project for an industry client. It taught me to work from incomplete plant data, state assumptions clearly, and give a recommendation a client can act on, including an honest note that the economics only work as a compliance cost.
