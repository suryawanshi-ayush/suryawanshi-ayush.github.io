At a glance

Problem
Platinum oxidises formic acid well at first, but adsorbed CO builds up and the current falls within an hour.
What I did
Made four Bi-modified Pt/C catalysts, tuned the catalyst ink, and tested them with and without a quinone mediator (AQSA).
Key result
Bi(1.25)-Pt/C, with the least bismuth, kept 92.9% of its current over a one-hour hold, against 58.5% for commercial Pt/C.
Limits
All results are from a three-electrode half-cell in acid. No membrane electrode assembly or full-cell test yet.

The question

Formic acid is an attractive fuel for small fuel cells: it is liquid, easy to store, and crosses a Nafion membrane far less than methanol does. Platinum oxidises it well at first, but a side reaction leaves carbon monoxide stuck to the surface. As the platinum fills up, the current drops within the first hour.

I tested two fixes, and whether they help or hinder each other. The first was decorating commercial Pt/C with bismuth, which is known to block the CO-forming path. The second was adding a dissolved quinone, anthraquinone-2-sulfonic acid (AQSA), as a redox mediator in the electrolyte.

What I did

  1. Made four catalysts

    Commercial 20 wt% Pt/C was soaked for 48 hours in bismuth nitrate at 1.25, 2.5, 5 and 10 mM in dilute sulphuric acid under nitrogen, then reduced with sodium borohydride and annealed at 300 °C under argon.

  2. Tracked where the bismuth went

    FE-SEM, EDX averaged over four spots per sample, powder XRD and, for the best catalyst, nitrogen physisorption (BET).

  3. Tuned the catalyst ink

    5 mg of catalyst in water and isopropanol, with either 5 µL or 20 µL of Nafion as binder.

  4. Built and tested electrodes

    For voltammetry and active-area measurements, 4 µL of ink was drop-cast on a polished glassy carbon disk (0.0707 cm²), the electrode in the photos. For the separate one-hour durability holds, the same ink was spread on 1 cm² carbon paper at the same loading, which gives a larger, more even area for long tests. Both were measured in a three-electrode cell with a platinum-coil counter and an Ag/AgCl reference on an Autolab potentiostat. AQSA was screened at 0, 1, 2 and 10 mM.

  5. Ran durability holds

    Each catalyst, on carbon paper, was held at 0.55 V vs RHE for 3600 s, with and without 1 mM AQSA.

What I found

Where the bismuth went

At the two lowest loadings there were no separate bismuth crystals in SEM or XRD: bismuth sat on the platinum as a thin decoration. At 10 mM, bright Bi-rich clusters appeared and covered much of the platinum.

Less Nafion, more current

On the glassy carbon electrode, the thinner Nafion film (5 µL per 5 mg of catalyst) gave 2.4–2.8× higher peak current than the thicker one (20 µL), even though the thicker film showed slightly more active area on plain Pt/C. The active-area measurement only needs protons to reach the platinum, but formic acid has to diffuse through the film, so a thick ionomer layer wins one test and loses the other.

Which catalyst lasted

The catalyst with the least bismuth lasted longest in both electrolytes. Adding 1 mM AQSA lowered the retention of every catalyst in this test, though with AQSA present Bi(1.25)-Pt/C gave the highest mass activity, 496.7 mA per mg of Pt.

One result surprised me. Bi(5)-Pt/C had the most impressive voltammogram, 851.7 mA per mg of Pt, but most of that peak came from oxidising CO that had already formed. It was recovering from poisoning rather than avoiding it, and kept only 52.8% of its current. On the activity that comes from the direct pathway, before any CO is burned off, Bi(1.25)-Pt/C led at 494.9 mA per mg of Pt, 5.5 times commercial Pt/C.

The measured curves behind these numbers

What it doesn’t show yet

Everything was measured in a three-electrode half-cell in acid, so these numbers rank catalysts against each other; they are not fuel-cell performance. The next steps written into the thesis are building a membrane electrode assembly with Bi(1.25)-Pt/C and testing it in a single cell, and using in situ spectroscopy to watch the bismuth layer while it works.

What I took from it

A good-looking voltammogram is not the same as a durable catalyst, and an active-area number is not the same as usable activity. I now check durability before ranking anything, and I think about what each measurement can and cannot see.
